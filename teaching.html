Teaching

Teaching Philosophy

The central goal of my philosophy teaching is the improvement of my students’ critical and analytical thinking. I want them to strengthen their reading and writing, sharpen their ability to identify logically valid arguments, develop their meta-cognitive abilities, and see the value of philosophy for themselves. I have pursued these goals across eleven courses as instructor of record at the University of Cincinnati and Eastern Michigan University, from large online sections of Introduction to Philosophy to Philosophy of Video Games. Four commitments organize how I teach.

Transparent alignment. I design learning goals and activities so that students can understand what I am asking them to learn, be confident in their ability to learn it, and see its value. I describe the idea with a weightroom analogy: ask a student to lift 200 pounds and they can verify the demand by checking the plates, judge whether the request is fair, and understand how lifting it will strengthen them. Assessment should approach that transparency, which is why I use concept maps in place of timed essay exams. Students generate the concepts relevant to a guiding question and draw the connections between them; when I re-use a question later in the term, they can see their own learning directly as the maps get bigger and more interconnected. Concept maps replace essay exams, not writing. My students write philosophy every class meeting.

Student-centered design. On the first day of every semester I ask students to write down their goals for the term, and I explain that the exercise is practice in meta-cognition. In all my courses I assign QCCRs (Question, Comment, Critique, Reflection) every day we meet: short handwritten pieces on the day’s material. They develop meta-cognition, and they show me daily how the course is landing. What I take to be the main point of a session and what students focus on are often different, and QCCRs let me adjust. A sharp student critique from Tuesday, put on the board Thursday with the author’s permission, is a better opening question than any I could script.

Maximally inclusive instruction. Students arrive with great variation in their backgrounds and their familiarity with college classroom norms, and my methods are designed for that variation. Students for whom English is a second language get frequent, fast, low-pressure feedback on their writing. Every student voices their thinking every day, including those uncomfortable speaking in front of the whole class. I have found the best inclusion strategy is flexibility with a spine: I allow late work and assessment retries where they create a learning opportunity the student would otherwise miss, and I assess narrowly on what my course itself teaches, so no exam question presupposes coursework a student never had the chance to take.

Teaching in the age of AI. As the Hastings Postdoctoral Scholar in AI and Humanity at Bowdoin, where my role includes building AI course tools with faculty across the disciplines, I have spent the past year on the question now sitting in every philosophy syllabus. My answer is that the productive question is not whether to permit AI but which uses of it develop the capacities a course targets and which substitute for them. Transparent alignment answers that question course by course. My own assessments were resilient to AI substitution before that was a design constraint, because they were built to make student thinking visible. Where AI serves a learning goal, I teach students to use it deliberately and critically. My students should leave a course better thinkers with AI than they would have been without it, and able to say why.

Courses Taught

Introduction to Philosophy

Introductory

A first encounter with philosophical thinking through enduring questions about knowledge, reality, morality, and the nature of mind. The course emphasizes close reading of primary texts and developing the skills of philosophical argumentation. Students learn to identify assumptions, construct arguments, and engage charitably with positions they find counterintuitive.

Featured assignment: The “philosophy reading ritual”—students borrow a philosophy book from the library and read it in a communal space like a coffee shop, reflecting on how the setting shapes their engagement with philosophical ideas.

Introduction to Ethics

Introductory

An introduction to moral philosophy through classical and contemporary frameworks: virtue ethics, deontology, consequentialism, and care ethics. The course moves between theoretical foundations and practical application, asking students to reason carefully about what they owe to others and why.

Contemporary Moral Issues

Intermediate

An issues-driven course examining contested moral questions in light of philosophical frameworks. Topics may include the ethics of AI and automation, climate justice, free speech, economic inequality, and emerging biotechnologies. Students develop the ability to analyze positions they initially find persuasive and to articulate positions they find uncomfortable.

Medical Ethics

Intermediate

An examination of ethical questions arising in medical practice and healthcare policy, including informed consent, end-of-life care, resource allocation, reproductive technologies, and the ethics of clinical research. The course draws on both philosophical theory and concrete cases to cultivate practical moral reasoning in clinical and policy contexts.

Philosophy Through Movies

Special Topics

This course uses cinema as a vehicle for philosophical inquiry, treating film as a medium capable of generating its own philosophical insights. Students analyze how narrative structure, visual composition, and the experience of spectatorship raise questions about personal identity, perception, free will, and the nature of reality.

Featured assignment: Students attend a film screening in a movie theater and write a reflection on how the communal, immersive experience of theatrical viewing differs from watching at home.

Philosophy of Video Games

Special Topics

An exploration of philosophical questions raised by and within video games: the nature of agency in interactive media, the ethics of virtual action, questions of identity in avatar-mediated experience, and the aesthetics of games as an art form. The course takes games seriously as objects of philosophical analysis while also using them as provocations for broader questions about choice, responsibility, and meaning.

Minds and Machines

Intermediate

An introduction to the philosophical foundations of the scientific study of the mind. The first two-thirds of the course traces cognitive science from its origins in the early twentieth century to the present, examining the major frameworks that have shaped how researchers understand thinking, perception, and intelligence. The final third turns to recent developments in artificial intelligence, including large language models and autonomous drone swarms, asking what these systems can and cannot tell us about minds.
