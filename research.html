Research

One question runs through all of my work: what do we learn by building things? I pursue it in three connected areas, asking what we learn by building in science, in the study of the mind, and in the practice of engineering itself.

Across all three areas I work with two kinds of tool at once: the careful conceptual analysis and logical argumentation of philosophy and the computational methods of artificial intelligence, including computer simulations, robots, and large-language-model text analysis.

Research Areas

Philosophy of Science

Maker’s Knowledge and Scientific Progress

The center of my program is an account of what makers know. The tradition running from Bacon through Vico’s verum-factum grounds the maker’s epistemic privilege in authorship: we know best what we make because we made it. I argue that authorship is the wrong ground. What gives making its epistemic force is severity, the world’s standing capacity to register failure in the maker’s performance. A robot that falls over corrects its builder in a way no argument can.

This grows out of my dissertation, Engineering Progress in Science (University of Cincinnati, 2025). The usual story says science advances by accumulating truer theories, new laws, and better explanations. I argue that this misses half of what scientists actually accomplish. The things they build, including instruments, models, calibration routines, and whole experimental systems, are achievements in knowledge in their own right, every bit as much as a new theory. Progress is the growth of what science can do as much as what it can say. I call this the operative account. In “Scientific Works and the Transfer of Technique” I give the account its unit: works are deliberate material interventions that durably extend the regularities a community can reliably exploit. That is why a technique like photolithography travels from microchip fabrication to experimental biology, across domains that share almost no theory.

Cognitive Science & AI

Cognitive Robotics and the Embodied Mind

Robots built to model cognition have a habit of outliving the theories they were built under. Grey Walter’s tortoises and Rodney Brooks’s subsumption architectures were separated by four decades and by the field’s sharpest theoretical dispute, the fight over whether cognition requires internal representation, yet both stayed scientifically productive through the decline of classical cognitivism. My explanation is that cognitive robots occupy what I call the instance role: unlike a model, which merely stands in for its target, they are built as candidate instances of the very kind of system the science aims to explain, and their builders answer to the world in the way severity demands.

Because the thesis concerns making, my method matches it. I evolve small artificial agents and measure what becomes of their neural dynamics when their coupling to the environment is severed, and I have built sensors modeled on the whiskers animals use to feel their way in the dark (the subject of my engineering master’s thesis). This work draws on a double training, a PhD in philosophy and a master’s in robotics and intelligent autonomous systems. I am now extending the account to machine learning, where we build systems whose behavior we cannot explain, and where I argue that opacity is evidence for the severity account rather than an objection to it.

Applied Ethics

The Ethics of Engineering

My third area grows directly out of my training as an engineer. I am developing an ethics of engineering built around professional identity. The core idea is that what an engineer ought to do is best understood through the kind of person the profession asks them to become, rather than through rules or a tally of costs and benefits alone. The first paper from this project, “An Identity-Based Ethics for Engineering,” is forthcoming in Science and Engineering Ethics. This carries into my teaching: in my ethics, medical-ethics, and moral-issues courses I encourage students to reflect on the role their identities play in shaping what ethical practices look like for them.

The same concern with how inquirers are formed runs through my work on teaching and on public engagement. My 2025 paper with Tim Elmo Feiten (“Leveraging Participatory Sense-Making and Public Engagement with Science for AI Democratization,” Studies in History and Philosophy of Science) argues that democratizing AI means restructuring what people can do with these systems and who participates in shaping them, not merely distributing finished products more widely. A volume under contract with Cambridge University Press, A Guide for Academic Researchers Conducting Science Outreach, turns this into practical advice for researchers doing public outreach.

Publications

Forthcoming
An Identity-Based Ethics for Engineering
Collin Lucken
Science and Engineering Ethics, forthcoming
Forthcoming
Engineering ethics education often relies on case-study methods focused on disaster prevention and professional codes. Recent work in virtue ethics and professional identity has begun to develop a possible alternative, but the philosophical foundations of engineering’s ethical character remain underdeveloped. This paper argues that engineering constitutes a morally significant professional identity: a life-shaping practice whose internal goods are oriented toward the development of central human capabilities. Drawing on Appiah’s identity framework, MacIntyre’s theory of practices and his account of compartmentalization, the capability approach as formalized by Robeyns, and Wolfendale’s regulative-ideal account of professional integrity, I analyze engineering identity through institutional analyses of France’s École Polytechnique and the United States’ Accreditation Board for Engineering and Technology (ABET). I argue that engineering’s normative regulative ideal is the development of human capabilities, and that this orientation provides the philosophical grounding for an identity-based approach to engineering ethics education that connects ethical development to the cultivation of engineering character.
When Does the Body Matter? A Dynamical Signature of Coupling Dependence in Evolved Neural Controllers
Collin Lucken
From Animals to Animats 18: Proceedings of the 18th International Conference on the Simulation of Adaptive Behavior (SAB 2026), Berlin. Springer Lecture Notes in Artificial Intelligence, forthcoming
Forthcoming
This paper presents a method for studying how continuous-time recurrent neural networks regulate behavior in virtual, highly idealized models of embodied, evolved agents, or ‘animats’. It asks the question: what patterns emerge in a simple animat’s neural dynamics when its sensorimotor coupling to the environment is severed or manipulated? The paper proposes three interventions that vary the incoming perturbations to the animat’s evolved neural controllers. It then applies these three interventions across 60 different controllers, evolved to find and move to a light source in their environment (i.e. phototaxis). The patterns of neural activity recorded in the animats during the phototaxis task are then measured using dynamical systems theory. Networks that react more to these interventions are described as those with greater embodiment dependence. In the analysis, embodiment dependence is found to be characterized by three largely independent dynamical dimensions: (1) input-driven amplification of neural activity, (2) distinctness of neural trajectories between trials, and (3) metastable-coordination richness (the number of transient coordination states the network visits).

Leveraging Participatory Sense-Making and Public Engagement with Science for AI Democratization
Collin Lucken & Tim Elmo Feiten
Studies in History and Philosophy of Science, Volume 110, 2025, pp. 55–64
Published
DOI
This article explores new potentials for productive dialogue between public engagement with science (PEWS) and radical embodied cognitive science (RECS). The concept of participatory sense-making from enactive cognitive science provides an account of why active, dialogical engagement in science communication is so effective. After establishing the connection between PEWS and RECS, the authors motivate the need for “participatory cognitive strategies” and present a case study showing their potential in actively involving different groups of stakeholders throughout the development of large-scale AI systems, contributing to ongoing debates about the meaning of “democratizing AI.”
Engineering Progress in Science
Collin Lucken
PhD Dissertation, University of Cincinnati, 2025
Completed
This dissertation develops an operative account of scientific progress that foregrounds practical cognition, material construction, and engineering alongside traditional intellectual reasoning. I argue that scientific works—including instruments, models, calibration routines, and experimental systems—are epistemically significant achievements that standard accounts of progress overlook. The framework reveals how engineering practices generate distinctive forms of knowledge and contribute to scientific progress on their own terms.
Under Review
Scientific Works and the Transfer of Technique
Collin Lucken
European Journal for Philosophy of Science
Under Review
Techniques routinely transfer between scientific domains that share no theoretical framework. Photolithographic patterning migrates from semiconductor physics to microfluidics; adaptive optics moves from astronomy to neuroscience; cryogenic vitrification transfers from materials science to structural biology. Existing philosophical accounts presuppose that cross-domain knowledge transfer in science requires theoretical or social mediation between the two domains. These accounts can be supplemented by increased attention to the material transformations scientific practice introduces to the environment. To that end, I introduce the concept of scientific works: deliberate material interventions that, by restructuring an organism-environment system, durably extend the set of invariants in that system that organism(s) can reliably exploit. Drawing on Gibson’s ecological psychology and niche construction theory, I argue that scientific works provide the best explanation of technique transfer. Transfer succeeds because scientific works expand science’s set of exploitable invariants (stable material regularities that persist across changes in theoretical perspective) and fails when there are no such exploitable invariants.
The Erotetic Logic of William Whewell’s Fundamental Antithesis
Collin Lucken
Studies in History and Philosophy of Science, resubmitted after revision
Under Review
William Whewell’s Fundamental Antithesis of Philosophy, the persistent opposition between conceptual pairs such as subject and object, theory and fact, ideas and sensations, is widely recognized as central to his philosophical system, yet its precise nature remains contested. Existing scholarship predominantly interprets the Antithesis through what I call the “hylomorphic view,” which understands it as a claim about how the mind imposes form upon sensory matter in the constitution of knowledge. This paper argues that the hylomorphic interpretation, while capturing important aspects of Whewell’s thought, obscures his primary concern: understanding the structure and progress of scientific inquiry. I propose an alternative “erotetic view” that interprets the Fundamental Antithesis through the logic of questions and answers. On this reading, the various antithetical pairs arise not from the metaphysics of mind-world relations but from the dynamics of inquiry itself—the questions scientists pose shape the answers they receive, and those answers in turn inform subsequent questions. Drawing on Whewell’s intellectual development as traced by Fisch, Snyder, and others, I show that his interest in the Antithesis emerged from his effort to explain the two-fold empirical and conceptual character of excellent science, not from Kantian epistemology. The erotetic framework connects Whewell to the Baconian tradition of “interrogating nature” and to contemporary work on erotetic logic by van Fraassen and Koralus. I demonstrate the interpretive advantages of this approach through a case study of Whewell’s account of induction, showing how the erotetic view explains why Whewell insists that induction comprises both the Explication of Conceptions and the Colligation of Facts—a claim that hylomorphic readings struggle to accommodate.
Mindshaping and Learner-Centered Pedagogy: A Revealing Harmony
Collin Lucken & W. John Koolage
Teaching Philosophy, resubmitted after revision
Under Review
The mindshaping research program, which is increasingly gaining attention in human social cognitive science, centers pedagogy as among the most important components of human cognition. By centering pedagogy as an essential social cognitive practice, the mindshaping research program is uniquely suited as a scientific framework for philosophy teaching. An explicit articulation of this connection demonstrates both the fruitfulness of the mindshaping research program and an opportunity for philosophy teachers to rethink or refine their teaching. This paper argues that philosophy teachers can usefully treat philosophy pedagogy as if it were a form of mindshaping. Our proposal is that adopting mindshaping as a ‘lens’ or regulative frame through which one conceives of philosophy teaching reorganizes what teachers see when they look at their own practice. Specifically, we show that viewing teaching through the lens of mindshaping conceived as a form of epistemic niche construction clarifies what good teaching aims to do: shape learners’ skills and dispositions to build and inhabit epistemic environments that extend their cognitive capacities.
In Preparation
Maker’s Knowledge in Science
Collin Lucken
In Preparation
Maker’s knowledge in science, a tradition running through Bacon, Hobbes, Vico, and recently reconsidered by Floridi (2018) and Currie (2022), has traditionally taken the form of a claim of epistemic privilege for makers: we know best what we make. In turn, that privilege has been defended on the grounds of authorship: we know best what we’ve authored. I develop an alternative reading that defends makers’ epistemic privilege on the grounds of material severity instead of authorship: making something can go wrong in many ways, so to have made something is to have learned much. The severity account I develop improves on the authorship account by explaining what it is about the activity of making, understood as embodied construction through material engagement, that is so epistemically generative.
Maker’s Knowledge in Machine Learning
Collin Lucken
In Preparation
The maker’s knowledge tradition holds that we know best what we make. Deep learning looks like a refutation of that claim: trained neural networks are among the most impressive manufactured artifacts in the history of technology, and the people who manufacture them are lacking in essential knowledge about why they behave the way that they do. I argue that machine learning opacity refutes only one particular account of the maker’s privilege, the authorship view, on which makers know what they make because they brought it about. On the alternative account I defend, the privilege flows from the material severity of making: the world’s standing capacity to register failure in the maker’s performance and thereby shape the maker’s reliability. Training a neural network is authorship without severity. The practitioner authors the model, but the optimization process absorbs the material sanction of the making. The severity account predicts what we see: that machine learning engineers know much about training techniques and system architectures, despite not knowing exactly how these networks produce the outputs they do.
